The playbook

Make the neutral
look partisan.

By law, civil servants must serve whichever government the country elects, and keep their own politics out of it.

Reform's words treat them as an opposition in waiting. They are “woke”. Their views need policing. They are expected to resist. And the answer at the top is political appointees.

That turns a neutral institution into a partisan one. Once senior officials owe their jobs to a party, they aren't impartial any more. The next government, of any colour, inherits a civil service built for someone else.

Meanwhile, the image of a “bloated” Whitehall hides who most civil servants actually are.

The reality

Who are
they, really?

54.9%

of civil servants work in operational delivery: paying benefits and pensions, running jobcentres, staffing prisons and issuing driving licences. Only 6.8% work in policy.

4 in 5

civil servants work outside London. The North West alone has more than 75,000.

£37,820

is the median civil service salary. In the most junior grades, it's £28,020.

557,825

people worked in the civil service in March 2026. Two-thirds of them work in five departments: Justice, Work and Pensions, HMRC, Defence and the Home Office.

Numbers have grown since 2016, when they were about 380,000, after cuts from around 480,000 in 2010.

2010

Parliament wrote civil service impartiality into law. Civil servants must be appointed on merit, through fair and open competition, and must serve the government of the day, whatever its politics.

The size and cost of the civil service is a fair argument. It has grown, and other parties have promised cuts too. Reform says its cuts would fall on back-office work such as policy, HR and communications, rather than the frontline. One civil service union has talked openly about striking against a Reform government, so Reform's concern isn't invented.

But cutting jobs is one thing. Deciding which political views are acceptable in the civil service, and putting party appointees in charge of it, is another.

The Institute for Government, which studies how government works, says there is little evidence that politically aligned officials run things better than career ones.

Reform describes a political enemy.

Most of them deliver frontline services, from pensions and jobcentres to prisons.
