The playbook

Blame the
messenger.

A lawyer's job is to argue a client's case under laws that Parliament passed. A judge decides who is right.

Reform's words skip both of those facts.

The lawyer becomes “left-wing”, as if taking the case were a political act. A legal right becomes something lawyers are “blocking”, as if the judge weren't there. Soldiers need protecting from lawyers, not from the laws the lawyers are applying.

This matters because blaming lawyers hides the real choice.

Reform's plan is to leave the European Convention on Human Rights, scrap the Human Rights Act, and set aside other treaties it calls roadblocks.

That's an argument about the law. It's easier to win if you make it about the lawyers.

The reality

Who actually
decides?

38%

of immigration appeals decided by a judge in April to June 2026 were allowed, meaning the Home Office decision didn't stand. For asylum appeals, it was 39%.

About 7,500 appeals were decided at a hearing or on the papers that quarter.

42%

of sampled Home Office asylum decisions in the twelve months to May 2025 had significant errors or failed quality checks.

National Audit Office figure, as reported by Free Movement.

14%

of immigration judicial review applications considered by judges in April to June 2026 were allowed to go ahead to a full hearing. The courts already filter out most challenges at the first stage.

rC29

is the barristers' cab-rank rule. A self-employed barrister must accept a case in their field whoever the client is, and whatever they think of the client or the cause.

Taking a case is a professional duty, not a political statement.

Lawyers have been targeted before. In September 2020, a man with a knife walked into Duncan Lewis, a London firm with a specialist immigration team, and demanded to see its head of immigration law. He said he had been prompted by a newspaper article about the firm's work. In 2024, a jury found he had prepared an act of terrorism and made threats to kill.

The attack came four days after the then Home Secretary blamed “activist lawyers” for frustrating deportations. The Law Society's president later called it the first example of a worrying new trend.

Some legal challenges are weak, and some come late and are frustrating. The system already filters them, and judges throw many out. You can argue that the law gives people too many grounds to appeal. That is an argument about the law, and it should be made in the open.

Reform blames the lawyer who reads out the law.

What it wants to change is the law itself.
