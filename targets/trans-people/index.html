The playbook

Make them
a threat.

The argument begins with something almost impossible to disagree with: children should be safe.

So should women.

There are legitimate debates about medicine for young people, single-sex services and how sex and gender should be treated in law.

But watch what happens to those debates.

A disputed medical treatment becomes “child abuse”.

A disagreement about policy becomes “gender ideology”.

A dispute over access to particular spaces becomes a question of keeping women safe.

And a minority of the population becomes associated, again and again, with danger to somebody else.

The political offer then becomes simple: you need protecting — and Reform will protect you.

The reality

Where's
the threat?

There are legitimate arguments about how the law should regulate single-sex spaces.

There are legitimate arguments about the treatment of children experiencing gender dysphoria.

And the Cass Review identified serious weaknesses in the evidence supporting medical interventions for children and young people.

But none of that establishes Reform's larger proposition.

Reform repeatedly places trans people and “gender ideology” inside a narrative about protecting children and keeping women safe.

So where is the evidence that trans people, as a group, pose a threat to either?

Puberty blockers?

The NHS stopped routinely commissioning puberty blockers for children and young people with gender dysphoria.

That followed serious concerns about the quality of the clinical evidence.

It is evidence of uncertainty about a medical treatment. It is not evidence that trans people are abusing children.

The PATHWAYS trial?

The trial exists precisely because important questions about puberty suppression need better evidence.

In February 2026, the medicines regulator raised concerns and recruitment was paused.

The protocol was then modified. The MHRA consulted independent experts and required strengthened safeguards covering matters including age, bone health, cognition, bleeding and information about fertility preservation.

In June 2026, the MHRA concluded that the modified protocol met the required regulatory standards. The Health Research Authority also approved it against its ethical standards.

Calling that regulated medical research “state-sanctioned child abuse” is Reform's characterisation. It is not the finding of the medicines regulator.

Women's spaces?

The Supreme Court has already resolved an important part of the legal dispute.

In April 2025 it unanimously held that “sex”, “man” and “woman” in the Equality Act 2010 refer to biological sex.

The Court also made clear that this does not remove the Equality Act's protections for trans people, including protection against discrimination because of gender reassignment.

The Act contains provisions governing separate and single-sex services. There are therefore real legal questions about how particular services and spaces should operate.

Those legal rules are not evidence that trans people collectively make women unsafe.

Schools?

What schools should teach children about sex and gender is a legitimate subject for public policy.

Governments can set guidance. Parents can disagree with it. Teachers, clinicians and politicians can argue about where the boundaries should lie.

But disagreement about what children should be taught is not evidence that trans people represent a danger to children.

Healthcare. Education. Single-sex services.

Three real policy questions.

Reform connects all three to the same emotional proposition:

Someone is threatening you and the people you love.

Reform will protect you from them.

You can debate every one of these policies without turning trans people into a threat.
