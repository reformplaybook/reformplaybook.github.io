The playbook

Turn people
into an army.

Look at the words Reform uses.

Invasion. Fighting-age. Our beaches. D-Day.

This is the language of war.

An army doesn't need to be understood. Nobody asks where an invading soldier came from, what he is running from, or whether he has a case. You just stop him.

But people who claim asylum are asking for protection. The asylum system exists to decide who qualifies and who doesn't.

Reform's language skips the decision and hands you the verdict. Each person becomes a “fighting-age male”, and every group becomes “unvetted men”.

Here is what the Home Office's own figures say about them.

The reality

An army?
Look closer.

6 in 10

people who crossed by small boat between 2018 and 2025, and have had a decision, were granted protection. That's 79,589 people granted, against 53,305 refused.

The figures cover initial decisions only; later appeals aren't included. Across all asylum claims, the grant rate fell to 38% in the year to June 2026.

28%

of people arriving by small boat in the year to June 2026 were women or children. The most common nationalities were Eritrean, Sudanese, Afghan, Somali and Iranian.

16th

is where the UK ranked in Europe for asylum claims per head of population, in the year to March 2026.

£49.18

a week is the support given to an asylum seeker in self-catered housing. It drops to £9.95 a week where meals are provided. Most asylum seekers aren't allowed to work while they wait.

Those waiting more than 12 months can apply for permission to work. Since March 2026, that permission is limited to graduate-level jobs.

17%

of asylum seekers receiving support were housed in hotels at the end of June 2026. That's 16,000 people, half as many as a year earlier.

“Unvetted”? Every asylum claimant is fingerprinted, photographed and put through mandatory identity and security checks, including checks against police records. Inspectors have criticised how well those checks were carried out in the past. That is a reason to fix the checks, not to pretend they don't exist.

Dangerous? UK research on migration and crime is limited. The studies that exist found no measurable effect on violent crime. One found that more asylum seekers in an area was linked to more property crime, and researchers have pointed to the ban on working as a possible reason.

Irregular migration is a real policy issue. So is the cost of the asylum system, and so is where people are housed. You can oppose a 1,250-bed site beside a village of 350 people, and many do.

But none of that turns people asking for protection into an army.

Reform describes an invasion.

The Home Office's figures describe people. Most of those who crossed by boat and got a decision were granted protection.
