The playbook

Decide who
really counts.

Reform doesn't say it's against disabled people. It says it will protect people with severe disabilities.

Then it draws a line.

On one side: broken backs and lost limbs. Disability you can see.

On the other: anxiety, mental illness, conditions that don't show. These become “the odd bout of anxiety”, a generation that won't get up for the alarm clock.

Then it asks you to look at your neighbour's closed curtains, and fill in the rest yourself.

But you can't see a disability through a window.

By Reform's own estimate, close to three million people would have their disability or sickness payments withdrawn or changed.

The reality

Look beyond
the curtains.

96%

of people who aren't working or looking for work mainly because of long-term sickness are disabled. If the neighbour behind the curtains is off sick long-term, they are almost certainly disabled.

5.5 million

disabled people are in work in the UK. PIP, the main disability benefit, can be paid whether someone works or not.

7.6%

of workless disabled people moved into work each year, compared with 28.3% of workless non-disabled people. That's about a quarter of the rate.

Average for 2014 to 2024. The employment gap between disabled and non-disabled people was 29.7 percentage points in 2025.

1.4%

of PIP spending was estimated to be lost to fraud in 2025/26.

That's up from 0.4% the year before, and the rise is real. It still means about 98.6% of PIP spending was not fraud.

“Broken backs and lost limbs”? PIP isn't based on which condition you have. It is based on how a condition affects your daily life.

“The odd bout of anxiety”? Government figures show that mental health conditions limit people's daily lives a lot about as often as back, neck and limb conditions do: 34% compared with 36%. For people with a mental illness or other nervous disorder, it's about half.

The rise in disability benefit claims is real, and every major party has plans to reform the system. Reform says it would keep support for the most severe cases and fund equipment and adaptations through local councils.

But the same party has pledged to repeal the Equality Act, the law that protects disabled people from discrimination at work, in shops, on transport and in public services.

A closed curtain tells you nothing about the person behind it. They may already work. They may be unable to work. They may be trying to get back into work, when disabled people move into jobs at about a quarter of the rate of everyone else.

Reform asks you to look at your neighbour's curtains.

We'd rather you looked at the evidence.
