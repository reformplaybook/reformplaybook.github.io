The playbook

Put the referee
on trial.

Look at an episode that came to light in May 2026.

In June 2025, while two men were on trial before a jury, Farage talked publicly about their case. The judge later wrote that the comments were potentially a contempt of court, because they implied the defendants were guilty.

He referred the matter to the Attorney General, who brought no proceedings. The judge also decided the trial could still go ahead fairly.

The judge wasn't taking sides. He was protecting the right to a fair trial by jury, the same right any voter would want if they were ever in the dock.

Farage's answer was that the judiciary is “in an even worse state than I imagined”.

That's the move. When a judge gets in the way, the judge becomes “activist”. And the fix on offer isn't a better argument in court. It's a new law designed so that judges can't stop deportations.

The reality

Who judges
the judges?

2005

Parliament passed the Constitutional Reform Act. It requires ministers to uphold the continued independence of the judiciary. The same Act created an independent commission to select judges on merit.

1.75%

of Crown Court sentences in 2025 were reported by victims or members of the public as possibly too lenient. That's 1,491 cases. The Attorney General can refer sentences covered by the scheme to the Court of Appeal, which can increase them.

When a judge gets it wrong, other judges can put it right.

1981

Under the Contempt of Court Act, publishing anything that creates a substantial risk of seriously prejudicing a live trial can be treated as contempt of court, whatever the intention. It applies to everyone, politicians included.

It exists to stop juries being swayed by anything other than the evidence.

What happens when judges are called political? The Lady Chief Justice told Parliament in 2025 that, after inaccurate reporting of a judgment, judges had been doorstepped and received death threats, and their families were living in fear. Her annual report that year described inaccurate and unfair criticism, sometimes personal, with security threats attached. A judicial security taskforce has since been set up.

None of this means judges are always right, or beyond criticism. The Lady Chief Justice has said so herself: judgments can and should be debated, and wrong ones can be appealed. Parliament can also change any law it thinks judges are applying badly.

What judges can't do is rule the way a politician wants because the politician says so. That's the point of having them.

Reform calls it activism.

The law calls it independence.
