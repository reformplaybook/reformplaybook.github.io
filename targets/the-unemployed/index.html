The playbook

Blame the person,
not the market.

Look at the words Reform uses.

Choosing. Mugs.

Both put the whole story inside one person's head. If someone isn't working, it's because they've decided not to, and you're the fool paying for it.

That story only works if the jobs are there, waiting.

It leaves out how many jobs there actually are. It leaves out how many people on benefits already work. And it leaves out the rules that already cut people's money if they don't look for work.

Then comes the offer: make them work for their benefits.

Britain has tried that before.

The reality

Where are
the jobs?

702,000

job vacancies in June to August 2026. Leaving aside the pandemic, that's the fewest since 2014. Around 1.77 million people were unemployed in April to June 2026, more than two for every vacancy.

The unemployment rate was 4.9%. The early estimate for August 2026 put the number of people on payrolls 145,000 lower than a year earlier.

50%

of people on Universal Credit are in the group not expected to work at present, for example because of a health condition, caring for someone, or having a child under one.

4.2 million of 8.3 million people, February 2026. Only 1.6 million were in the group that must search for work, and a third of those already had some earnings.

37.8%

of people on Universal Credit were already in work in January 2026. That's 3.1 million people.

£424.90

a month is the Universal Credit standard allowance for a single person aged 25 or over in 2026/27. That's about £98 a week.

0.4%

of people on the main unemployment benefit were sanctioned for refusing a job in November 2024. That's about 7,500 people out of 2.1 million.

That month, 117,000 had payments cut for all sanction reasons combined. The rules to penalise people who won't look for work already exist. Figures are the Centre for Social Justice's analysis of DWP data, as reported by The Telegraph.

Work for your benefits? Britain tried a version of this with Mandatory Work Activity, launched in 2011. The Department for Work and Pensions' own analysis found it had only a small, short-lived effect on benefit claims, and no effect on whether people found jobs. Some people referred to it ended up moving onto sickness benefits instead. (The analysts noted these estimates were less robust than for some other schemes.)

Some people who could work don't. Every benefits system has them, and the rules already allow jobcentres to cut their money.

But Reform's story needs you to believe that's the main reason people are out of work. It isn't what the figures show. They show fewer vacancies, falling payrolls, and millions of people on benefits who are already working, ill or caring for someone.

Reform says they're choosing not to work.

The jobs figures say there aren't enough jobs to choose from.
