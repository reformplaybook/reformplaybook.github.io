The playbook

Turn neighbours
into a bill.

Look at the words Reform uses.

Wave. Burden. Millstone. Live off the state.

Every person becomes a cost.

These aren't people who crossed the Channel. They came on visas, under rules the government set, mostly to work or to join family. In most cases they can't claim benefits while they wait.

After years of following the rules, they can apply to settle. Reform's plan is to abolish that right, including for many people who already have it, and make them reapply under much stricter conditions.

To justify it, Reform put a price on them: £234 billion.

Here's what happened to that number.

The reality

Check
the bill.

£234bn

was Reform's headline cost for these migrants. It came from a Centre for Policy Studies report that the think tank withdrew after a dispute over the numbers. The think tank says the figure should no longer be used.

£47bn

is the government's migration advisers' estimate of what one year's Skilled Worker arrivals (2022/23) will contribute to the public finances over their lifetimes, after costs.

Not every group pays its way. The Home Office estimates that care workers and their adult dependants settling between 2026 and 2030 will cost about £10bn net over their lifetimes. That's a real figure, and far smaller than the one Reform used.

5 years

is how long most people on work and family visas wait before they can apply to settle. Until then, they generally can't claim benefits. Settlement requires English, the Life in the UK test and a clean criminal record.

1 in 5

NHS staff in England report a nationality other than British.

171,000

was net migration in 2025. That's nearly half the 2024 figure, and the lowest level since the new immigration system began in 2021.

ONS provisional estimate; these are official statistics in development.

A burden? It depends who you mean. Higher-paid workers tend to put in more than they take out. Some lower-paid routes and dependants cost more than they contribute. That is a real policy question, and the government has already consulted on making people wait longer and earn settlement.

“Reject our way of life”? People who settle here have to pass an English requirement and a test on British life and values, and keep a clean criminal record.

Reform's plan goes further than tightening the rules for newcomers. It would reach back to people who were told that if they worked, paid in and obeyed the law for five years, they could stay. Lawyers have warned it could be challenged in the courts, and the care sector has warned of staff shortages.

Reform shows you a bill.

Look again, and some of them are working on your hospital ward.
